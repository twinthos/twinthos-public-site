Two million prompts, three breached providers, nobody knew

Canary credentials sent through AI inference services came back used. Zero Data Retention did not survive the first hop.

In brief

The experiment

Dragos ran a controlled study between July and November 2026. It sent approximately two million prompts through widely used AI services and routing layers. Each prompt contained a unique URL and a canary credential — a key that exists only inside that one request, so any later use of it proves the request data was copied.

The design is what makes the result usable. It does not rely on a provider admitting a breach or a customer noticing a bill. It waits for the bait to be taken, then traces where it was taken from.

What was taken

Data from at least three providers was exposed, and none of the three knew about it before Dragos notified them. The largest breach, in late October 2026, involved a couple of hundred AWS keys. Every request sent during the affected windows was stolen, including requests the provider had rejected before a model ever ran.

The stolen keys were then used for tens of thousands of AWS API calls. The attacker listed S3 buckets, searched Secrets Manager, and tried to create Bedrock API keys and IAM users. That is not an academic result: it is an attacker treating an inference provider's logs as an entry point into a cloud account.

Zero retention did not hold

In two of the three breaches, some of the stolen requests carried the Zero Data Retention flag. Twenty-four days after one such request, a secret link from that prompt was posted into a Feishu chat channel and opened from six devices in Beijing and Shanghai — from a request sent to a US provider with the flag set.

OpenRouter responded by off-boarding three providers and removing 125 endpoints from Zero Data Retention eligibility, and stated its own systems were not breached. The flag did what a contract can do: it bound the party that signed it. It did not bind whatever wrote the log downstream.

The reporting gap

The UK regulator cannot currently count this class of incident. In a February 2026 response to a request, the ICO confirmed it has no incident-type category for AI systems, received 12,412 data breach reports in 2024/25, and has no current plans to separate AI-related incidents from other breaches.

So a leaked prompt containing pricing, code or a client's contract is invisible to the regulator unless it also triggers a personal-data notification. That does not make the leak harmless. It makes it unmeasured, which is worse for a board trying to decide whether to care.

IN PRACTICE / WHAT THE RECORD SHOWS

What the record shows, and what it does not

The numbers that can be checked are precise: approximately two million prompts, at least three providers, a couple of hundred AWS keys, tens of thousands of API calls from the bait, 125 endpoints removed from retention eligibility, and 24 days before one link was opened from six devices.

What cannot be checked is the part most buyers want: no provider is named. Dragos says at least three were breached; OpenRouter says it off-boarded three. There is no published evidence that the two lists are the same set, so any story naming a vendor is a guess dressed as reporting.

THE OPERATING DECISION

Five questions before routing client data through a marketplace

Which provider actually serves each request, and will you tell me when that changes. What is stored, for how long, and on whose storage. Which subcontractors see the request, and are they on a list I can read. What evidence of deletion can you give me — not a policy, evidence. And if a key from my request were used elsewhere, would you be able to tell me within a week.

Then price the alternative honestly. Running inference through a single provider under your own contract, or self-hosting a smaller model for sensitive text, usually costs more per token and less per incident. The decision is not about tokens.

DIRECT ANSWER

Are my prompts stored by AI providers?

Often yes, and the retention setting you chose may only govern the provider you bought from. In this study, prompts marked for zero retention were among the data stolen from at least three providers, and one link from such a prompt was opened from six devices 24 days later. The workable assumption is that any prompt you send may be stored by someone, then design what you send accordingly.

THE PRACTICAL TEST

Treat every prompt as a document that leaves the building. Put client identifiers, contract text and credentials behind a filter, not a retention flag, and ask each vendor for evidence of deletion rather than a policy page.
