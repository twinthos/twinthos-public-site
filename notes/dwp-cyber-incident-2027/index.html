DWP cyber incident: the portal died at nine, and a person became the plan

At nine this morning the Universal Credit journal wouldn't load. A fit note was due. Someone's rent depended on a change they could not report. The page said temporary disruption and offered nothing else.

Tyson Mandela · Published 27 November 2026 · 6 min read

By lunchtime, every cyber firm in Britain had posted the same paragraph about resilience. Not one of them mentioned the obvious thing. When the portal is down, their software is down too.

What DWP actually confirmed

DWP took some online services offline as a precaution after identifying a cyber incident affecting its systems. Universal Credit and the DWP job search went dark. The National Cyber Security Centre and the Government Cyber Coordination Centre are investigating. Payments are unaffected, according to the department's statement.

That is the full official account. DWP has not said which systems were touched, what was accessed, whether data left the building, or when the service returns. Anyone who tells you otherwise is guessing, and several people already are.

The distinction matters. "Incident" is not "attack". "Offline as a precaution" is not "contained". A department that takes everything down at once is telling you it could not see what was moving inside its own network.

The cost that outlives the outage

Money moves on a schedule. A claim changes when a human reports a change. New job, new address, someone moving in, a hospital stay. The portal is where that report goes.

While it is dark, the change does not exist. A change that does not exist can come back months later as an overpayment, then a recovery letter, then a deduction from money already spent on rent. DWP will restore the service. It will not restore the six weeks when nobody could tell it anything.

And the fallback was not a system. It was a person. The adviser with six open cases and no status page. The council officer with a queue and no script. Citizens Advice bureaux and council welfare rights teams answered the calls the portal could not. Nobody warned them. Nobody gave them a number to ring. They became the contingency plan at nine o'clock and they will be again tomorrow.

Payments are unaffected is a press release. It is not a plan.

2027 gets faster, not smarter

Here is the part worth reading twice. The NCSC says the weaknesses being exploited in these campaigns are not new, and the tools provide no capability an ordinary attacker did not already have. What changed is speed, persistence and cost. Since September 2026 the NCSC has recorded a marked rise in AI-assisted attacks using agentic offensive frameworks, in its own words.

Its assessment to 2027 says AI will almost certainly keep compressing the gap between a vulnerability being published and being exploited, a gap already measured in days. By 2027 it expects AI to sharpen attacks on known vulnerabilities and to widen the attack surface as more AI sits inside critical systems with weak controls. That is a forecast, from the NCSC, not a headline.

So the 2027 problem is not robots inventing attacks nobody has seen. It is your unpatched server, your reused password, your agent's standing access, hit faster and more often than your team can answer the phone.

Five problems, five owners

Standing access. An agent gets connected with one person's permissions and stays connected after that person leaves. Revoking the human login does not revoke the token. Fix: a named account for the agent, the narrowest access that does the job, revocation on the last day, not the following month.

Instructions hidden in documents. An invoice, a CV or a grant application can carry text a model reads as an order. The person sees a document. The agent sees a task, and it already has the mail and payment access to finish it. Fix: keep irreversible actions, payments and deletions, outside the agent's reach.

Actions nobody watches. Work runs overnight with logs sitting in a vendor console you never open. Fix: logs in your own system, a weekly sample of what it did, and a stop switch you have actually tested.

Suppliers you already trust. The intruder often arrives through a tool or provider that already holds your keys, then works with valid credentials. Fix: a written supplier list and out of band verification for any change to bank details or access.

Memory you cannot audit. Assistants keep history and embeddings in someone else's environment, with no retention rule you wrote. Fix: decide what may be remembered, where it lives and how it is deleted, before a subject access request asks you.

Notice what these have in common. None of them is solved by buying another tool. All of them are solved by somebody owning a decision.

What AI does not change

Patch known flaws. Kill default credentials. Close the services you forgot were public. Keep logs you can read. Give people the least access that works.

The NCSC's point is that the fundamentals still hold. An agent speeds up the attack, not the defence. Only you can close a door.

The one hour test

Ask your team one question. If every portal we use died at nine, what could we still do by lunch?

If the answer is nothing, you have a login screen and a hope, not a plan.

Then write one page. Which tasks stop. Who does them by hand. Which record goes stale first. Who checks the official status at nine. Where the paper copy lives.

Keep proof where the outage cannot reach it. Last statement, reference numbers, a dated screenshot. When systems go dark, the only argument you can still win is the one with paper behind it.

What I would do about it

I build AI agents for UK firms, and I will say the part that costs me. When your portal is fully offline, my agents stop too. They cannot log into a dead system, and any vendor who tells you otherwise is guessing.

What they can do is narrower and worth paying for. Find the cases that went stale while the system was down. Draft the note that records what changed, with the evidence attached and the sign-off named. Keep the job spec and the history outside the portal, so the work does not vanish with the login. Then a named person approves anything that moves money or changes a claim.

If you want a second pair of eyes before 2027 lands, send me three lines. The system you cannot run without. The person who owns it. What still works at nine when it dies. I will reply with the one page test and the questions your incident review will ask.

Questions people are asking

Is Universal Credit down? It was on 27 November 2026, along with the DWP job search. DWP says benefit and pension payments are still being made.

Was data stolen? Not confirmed. DWP has not said what was accessed, and nobody outside the response knows.

Is this an AI attack? Unknown. DWP has not named a cause. The NCSC's point is that AI is making ordinary attacks faster, not that it invented this one.

What should a small business do first? Write the one page. Name the owner. Test the stop switch. Do it on a quiet day, because this week is not quiet.

Name the owner before the portal comes back.
