Who switches off Universal Credit? Five roles, no AI agent list

At 19:20 on Thursday the reports started. By nine the DWP estate was coming down. By Friday afternoon five people owned five different questions, and none of them could answer the smallest one: which software with credentials was already running inside the department.

What DWP did, in the order it did it

DWP took its own services offline as a precaution, and it published that decision. The sequence is on the record, and it doesn't need a vendor to explain it.

  • 19:20 to 21:00, Thursday 1,300 reports to Downdetector against a normal rate of about one every 15 minutes (BBC Verify, 27 November 2026).
  • 08:11, Friday DWP publishes its notice: some services offline as a precaution, payments unaffected (GOV.UK).
  • 12:16 Number 10 calls it a live and ongoing incident, says there is currently no evidence of customer data loss, and cannot rule out a malicious actor (BBC live coverage).
  • 13:48 McFadden calls it a serious cyber incident and says there is no evidence of anyone's personal details being compromised.
  • 15:41 More than 15,000 problem reports on gov.uk, about two thirds about login (BBC Verify).
  • Not published A restoration date. Universal Credit had 8.4 million claimants in August 2026 (DWP quarterly statistics, 17 November 2026).

Every firm in Britain posted the same paragraph about resilience. I want to look at the switch instead. Somebody decided to take the estate offline, and that decision has an owner, a time and a paper trail.

The Secretary of State owns the sentence

He has to say something true and short, on camera. Pat McFadden called it "a serious cyber incident" and said there was no evidence of anyone's personal details being compromised (Guardian live coverage, 27 November 2026).

That's the safest sentence available at 13:48. Two facts would have made it stronger: whether data left, and when the service returns. He didn't have either. Ministers get judged on the second one, because a date is a promise and a promise can be checked.

Watch the wording. No evidence isn't the same as no. An adviser who lets a minister collapse that distinction is buying a correction later.

The Permanent Secretary owns the letter nobody reads until later

The accounting officer signs that public money was spent properly and risk was managed, under Managing Public Money. After an incident that signature turns into a written explanation for the National Audit Office and the Public Accounts Committee.

The question is narrow. Who knew what, on what date, and why was resilience not funded. The evidence is a risk register with dates, a fallback that was tested, and a list of systems with named owners.

The CDIO owns the restore order

Restoration isn't one switch. Identity, payments, case management and notifications lean on each other, and the order you bring them back in tells you what the department thinks is safe.

If the death notification service returns last, the concern is data moving between departments, not one broken portal. That is a different investigation with a different owner.

The security lead owns the proof

Containment has to be demonstrated. That means how they got in, what was touched, what left, and how anyone knows.

Government measures this against GovAssure, which maps to the NCSC Cyber Assessment Framework. A supplier who can't map its claims to that framework is noise in an incident room, however good the demo was. That's the part the demo skips.

The CFO owns the bill that arrives after the outage

Payments kept going out. The cost doesn't sit there. It sits in the changes nobody could report: a fit note not submitted, a wage change not entered, an overpayment created in November and recovered in March. Nobody's budget covered that in advance.

The comparators are specific. Co-op lost at least £206m in revenue after a breach that took data on 6.5 million members (BBC News, 25 September 2025). Capita was fined £14m over 6.6 million people (ICO, 15 October 2025). Synnovis cost £32.7m and delayed more than 11,000 appointments (NHS England). WannaCry cost the NHS £92m (DHSC written answer 80987, 21 October 2025).

Those are four different incidents and four different counts. Read each number for what it counts, not for what it suggests.

The list nobody had

Here's the gap I care about, and it isn't a DWP problem. Most large estates now run software that acts on its own: scripts holding tokens, chatbots with database access, agents that send email as a named person.

At nine at night, with the estate coming down, you need one page. What acts, as whom, and who signed for it. When the access expires.

A department that could switch itself off in an evening couldn't produce that page by Friday afternoon. Neither could most banks, insurers or software firms I look at. The list doesn't exist because nobody was ever asked to keep it, and because the person who buys the agent is rarely the person who owns the risk.

What Twinthos would and wouldn't have done

Would: hold the job spec and the approved knowledge outside the portal, in the Company Brain, so a person can pick the work up by hand the same morning. Draft the note for each unreported change, with a work log and one named approver. Keep an agent passport for every agent we are told about: owner, purpose, tools, data sources, sensitive actions, expiry, approver.

Wouldn't: reopen a portal, detect an intrusion, restore a system, or find shadow AI that nobody showed us. When the portal is fully offline, our agents stop too. Anyone selling continuity inside a dead system is selling a diagram.

That limit is the honest half of the offer. An agent can hold the thread of work, and it can write down what changed. It can't be the service, and it won't pretend to be.

What to ask before the next one

Two hours with your own team answers more than any vendor meeting, and it doesn't cost a penny.

If every portal went down at nine tonight, what could we still do by lunch? Which of our agents hold credentials, and who signed for each one? What's the restore order, and who decides it?

Write the answers down. The first is a fallback plan. The second is an inventory. The third is a decision you'll have already made once, on a quiet day, instead of at nine at night.

Questions people are asking

Is Universal Credit working again? No restoration timetable had been published as of 27 November 2026. DWP says benefit and pension payments are unaffected.

Was data stolen from DWP? No official statement confirms it. Number 10 said there's currently no evidence of customer data loss. Absence of evidence isn't a finding.

Was the DWP incident ransomware? No official statement has said so. I won't assert it, and neither should anyone selling you a control.

What should a board ask first? The questions above, starting with the list of agents that hold credentials. It costs an hour and no budget.

Name the owner before the next portal goes down.
