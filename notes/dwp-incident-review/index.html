DWP cyber incident: 8.4 million claimants, one sign-in page returning nothing

DWP took services offline at 08:11 and called it a precaution. At 17:40, from this machine, the Universal Credit sign-in still answered a connection with silence. Twelve slides on the record, the 12 hours 55 minutes before it, and the one number nobody has published.

Tyson Mandela · Published 27 November 2026 · 12 slides, about 4 minutes

At 08:11 the department published three sentences: some online services taken offline as a precaution after a cyber incident, the National Cyber Security Centre and the Government Cyber Coordination Centre investigating, benefits and pensions still being paid.

By the noon Downing Street briefing, a malicious actor had not been ruled out. Later the work and pensions secretary called it a serious cyber incident. As at 17:40 there was no named cause, no statement on whether any data left, and no restoration date.

I ran four requests against four addresses twice, an hour and a half apart. The result did not change. The slides below are that record, set out in order, with what I could verify and what I could not.

What I ran, and what it returned

From one Linux host: the Universal Credit sign-in, the new claims service, Tell Us Once, and dwp.gov.uk. The first completed a TLS handshake and then sent zero bytes until the request timed out. The second and third did not resolve at 16:10, which means the public name had no address, not that the server refused. dwp.gov.uk answered normally.

That is four observations, repeated. It is not a diagnosis, and I have not offered one.

What none of this proves

It does not prove an intrusion, and it does not prove there was none. It does not prove data left or stayed. The pattern, a service that accepts a connection and returns nothing while sibling names vanish from DNS, is consistent with a deliberate shutdown, which is also what DWP described. Consistency is not evidence of a cause.

The one thing a reader can check is the timing: first public reports of the failure at 19:16 the night before, the department's first statement published at 08:11. Twelve hours and fifty-five minutes in which the page was failing and nothing was said publicly.

The clock that starts at knowledge

If a breach could affect people's rights, UK GDPR gives 72 hours from the moment the organisation knows. That clock starts at knowledge, not at the end of the investigation, which is earlier than any public statement. "No evidence of loss" is a position, and a position needs a record behind it.
