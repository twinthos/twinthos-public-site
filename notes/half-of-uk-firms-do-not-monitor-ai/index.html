72% of UK firms use AI. Half do not monitor what it does.

Two November surveys and one breach report put a number on the gap between using AI and being able to see it.

Twinthos · Published 27 November 2026 · 27 November 2026 · 4 min read

THE SHORT ANSWER

In brief

The British Chambers of Commerce found 72% of UK firms using AI in November 2026, up from 54% in March, across more than 1,300 leaders, 87% of them SMEs. Half of those firms say they do not monitor their AI usage at all, and only 3% run platforms that decide and execute. The government's own breach survey found around a quarter of AI-using businesses have cyber security practices in place to manage AI risk. IBM puts the cost of an AI-enabled breach at $6m against a $4.99m global average and reports that 92% of organisations breached through AI lacked proper AI access controls.

The adoption number

The British Chambers of Commerce published its survey on 24 November 2026, with fieldwork from 6 October to 4 November and more than 1,300 leaders responding, 87% of them from SMEs. 72% said they use AI, against 54% in March 2026. Twelve per cent had built something bespoke.

The same survey found the shape of that use: 48% use AI ad hoc, and only 3% run full-spectrum platforms that decide and execute. Security, privacy and compliance were named by 40% as the biggest barrier to going further — which makes confidence, not appetite, the constraint on the next stage.

The monitoring gap

Half of the firms surveyed said they do not monitor their AI usage at all. Thirty-nine per cent actively monitor. Thirty-one per cent have never considered it.

The government's Cyber Security Breaches Survey 2025/26, published on 30 April 2026 and based on 2,112 UK businesses, measured a nearby thing: of businesses using, adopting or considering AI, around 24% had cyber security practices or processes in place to manage AI risk. Two different methods, one conclusion. Use is normal; oversight is rare.

What a breach looks like when nobody watches

IBM's 2026 Cost of a Data Breach report puts the global average at $4.99m, a 12% rise and a record. One in four malicious breaches was AI-enabled, and those cost $6m on average. More than 20% of organisations reported a breach that targeted AI models or applications.

The mechanisms were ordinary: compromised APIs, applications or plug-ins at 27%, and cloud misconfiguration affecting AI workloads at 27%. Among organisations that had an AI-related breach, 92% lacked proper AI access controls, and only 40% of all organisations use access controls on AI models and data. Mean time to identify and contain rose to 247 days. The UK average was £3.13m, with 22% of UK organisations reporting AI-generated attacks.

What closing it is worth

IBM prices the gap precisely. Organisations using security AI and automation extensively averaged $4.00m per breach, against $5.93m for those using none — a $1.93m difference — and contained incidents 65 days faster (215 days against 280). Yet only 36% of breached organisations used those tools extensively, and in the UK the figure is 32%.

The allocation is the more useful finding. Half deploy agents in the security operations centre, and more than half of those point them at threat hunting, response and containment. Just 18% use agents for vulnerability scanning and management — the exposure that automated attackers now find first.

IN PRACTICE / WHAT THE RECORD SHOWS

What the record shows, and what it does not

These surveys measure what firms say about themselves. The BCC figures come from leaders who chose to respond; the government survey sampled 2,112 businesses by telephone. Neither measures how much data is actually leaving, and neither can see the free tools a single employee signed up for on a Friday.

What they do establish is a consistent direction across independent methods: use has doubled in under a year, monitoring has not, and the breaches that follow are concentrated in access control and configuration rather than in model quality.

THE OPERATING DECISION

The four-line register that answers the board question

Line one: the tool and who pays for it. Line two: the worst data it can receive — client names, contracts, staff records, nothing. Line three: whether that data is needed, and what replaces it if not. Line four: who reviews the entry, and how often.

Then add one control rather than a framework: remove standing access to client data from any tool that does not need it, and check the configuration of the accounts that remain. IBM's numbers say the money sits in access control and configuration, not in buying another detection product.

DIRECT ANSWER

How many UK businesses actually use AI in 2026?

It depends which official measure you take, and the difference is worth knowing. The British Chambers of Commerce put it at 72% in November 2026 across its own membership sample. The government's Cyber Security Breaches Survey puts the figure near 31% for businesses using, adopting or considering AI, and ONS measured around 35% of businesses with 10 or more employees in June 2026. Different samples, different questions. The direction is the same in all three.

THE PRACTICAL TEST

Build the four-line register this week and name one person who owns it. Then remove standing access to client data from every tool that does not need it. That is a day of work, and it addresses the two causes IBM finds in more than half of AI-related breaches.
