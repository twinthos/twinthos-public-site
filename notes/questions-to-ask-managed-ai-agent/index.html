Before you give an agent access.

Questions that turn an impressive demonstration into a reviewable deployment.

THE SHORT ANSWER

In brief

Ask to see the boundary, the failure and the recovery. Not just the successful run.

What can it reach?

Ask for a concrete list of accounts, applications, folders and actions. “It connects to your tools” is not a permission model. A system that reads customer records does not automatically need permission to export them. Review access with the person responsible for each system.

Who can tell it what to do?

A request from the owner and text found on a webpage should not have the same authority. Prompt injection can arrive through external material the agent reads. Ask how the application limits the consequences of that material influencing the model. OWASP describes this as indirect prompt injection.

What needs a person?

Choose the actions that require approval: external messages, record changes, spending or unusual cases. Name the approver and decide what happens if they are unavailable. A button labelled “approve” is only useful if the action cannot bypass it.

How do you know it worked?

Ask to inspect a successful run, a failed run and a rejected action. What was attempted? What changed? Which evidence confirms the result? Establish who receives an alert and how to pause the workflow. Include a recovery exercise before accepting the deployment.

What changes after launch?

Find out who maintains credentials, integrations, source material and tests. Agree which changes require a fresh review. Finally, ask what you can export when you leave: your knowledge, configuration, records and operating documentation should be discussed before signing.

IN PRACTICE / ILLUSTRATIVE SCENARIO

A permission question is a business question

Imagine an assistant that drafts client replies. Its demo shows a polished email, but the real risk is the difference between reading a client file, drafting a reply and actually sending it. Ask for those three permissions separately. Then introduce an incoming email that contains a request to forward the entire customer database. The request came from the email, not the responsible business owner. A defensible system should treat it as untrusted content and refuse a data export that is outside the approved job. The evidence worth seeing is the attempted tool call and the application's decision, not just a reassuring sentence from the model.

THE OPERATING DECISION

What a credible acceptance review looks like

Ask for a list of tool endpoints, effective permissions and external destinations. Choose a successful task, a denied task and a partial failure. For each, identify the trigger, the authorised identity, the decision point and the actual state of the external system afterwards. Approval must be meaningful: the reviewer needs to see the action, its scope and its consequences before allowing it. A screenshot of an approval button without enforcement evidence proves little. Finally, document who can stop the system, restore access and revoke credentials. This does not eliminate risk; it makes specific risks reviewable and manageable.

DIRECT ANSWER

What proves an AI agent is safe enough to deploy?

Not a single score or a prompt promising good behaviour. The relevant evidence is a defined scope, tested permissions, monitored execution, recovery procedures and human acceptance of the remaining risks.

THE PRACTICAL TEST

Choose one real action, such as sending a reply. Ask the provider to show the allowed scope, a denied attempt and the audit record.
